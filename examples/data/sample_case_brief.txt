CASE BRIEF: BROWN v. BOARD OF EDUCATION OF TOPEKA

Citation: 347 U.S. 483 (1954)

PARTIES:
- Plaintiff/Appellant: Oliver Brown, et al.
- Defendant/Appellee: Board of Education of Topeka, Kansas, et al.

PROCEDURAL HISTORY:
This case consolidated four cases from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, and Delaware. The district courts ruled in favor of the school boards based on the "separate but equal" doctrine established in Plessy v. Ferguson, 163 U.S. 537 (1896). Plaintiffs appealed directly to the Supreme Court.

FACTS:
African American children were denied admission to schools attended by white children under laws requiring or permitting segregation according to race. The Negro and white schools were equalized, or in the process of being equalized, with respect to buildings, curricula, qualifications and salaries of teachers, and other "tangible" factors.

ISSUE:
Does segregation of children in public schools solely on the basis of race, even though the physical facilities and other "tangible" factors may be equal, deprive the children of the minority group of equal educational opportunities?

HOLDING:
Yes. Racial segregation in public education violates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

REASONING:
Chief Justice Warren, writing for a unanimous Court, held that:

1. Education is perhaps the most important function of state and local governments. Compulsory school attendance laws and the great expenditures for education demonstrate our recognition of the importance of education to our democratic society. As stated in Meyer v. Nebraska, 262 U.S. 390 (1923), education is required for the performance of our most basic public responsibilities.

2. To separate children in grade and high school from others of similar age and qualifications solely because of their race generates a feeling of inferiority as to their status in the community that may affect their hearts and minds in a way unlikely ever to be undone.

3. "Whatever may have been the extent of psychological knowledge at the time of Plessy v. Ferguson, this finding is amply supported by modern authority." The Court cited social science research demonstrating the harmful psychological effects of segregation on African American children.

4. "We conclude that, in the field of public education, the doctrine of 'separate but equal' has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal."

RULE OF LAW:
State-imposed segregation in public education violates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, regardless of whether facilities are physically equal. The "separate but equal" doctrine of Plessy v. Ferguson is overruled as applied to public education.

SIGNIFICANCE:
1. This landmark decision overturned Plessy v. Ferguson's "separate but equal" doctrine in the context of public education.

2. The decision paved the way for integration and became a catalyst for the civil rights movement.

3. The case established that separate facilities are inherently unequal when mandated by the state.

4. It led to the Civil Rights Act of 1964, 42 U.S.C. § 2000d, which prohibits discrimination in federally funded programs.

RELATED CASES:
- Plessy v. Ferguson, 163 U.S. 537 (1896) - Established "separate but equal" doctrine (overruled by this case)
- Bolling v. Sharpe, 347 U.S. 497 (1954) - Companion case applying similar principles to District of Columbia schools under the Fifth Amendment
- Cooper v. Aaron, 358 U.S. 1 (1958) - Reaffirmed Brown and held that state governments must obey federal court orders
- Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education, 402 U.S. 1 (1971) - Upheld busing as a means to achieve desegregation

SUBSEQUENT HISTORY:
In Brown v. Board of Education (Brown II), 349 U.S. 294 (1955), the Court addressed implementation, ordering desegregation to proceed "with all deliberate speed." This led to decades of litigation over school desegregation remedies.

NOTES:
1. The unanimous decision gave the ruling additional moral authority and legitimacy.
2. Chief Justice Warren worked to achieve unanimity, recognizing the case's national importance.
3. The decision relied heavily on sociological and psychological evidence, a somewhat controversial approach at the time.
4. Implementation proved difficult, with many states resisting integration for years.
